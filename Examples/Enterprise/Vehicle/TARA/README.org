* Scope and sources

[[https://www.iso.org/standard/70918.html][ISO/SAE 21434]] defines lifecycle
cybersecurity risk management for road-vehicle E/E systems; it does not
prescribe one technology or policy language. [[https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2025/5/oj/eng/pdf][UN R155 Annex 5]]
provides a baseline of threats and corresponding mitigations to consider
in vehicle risk assessment. [[https://openstd.samr.gov.cn/bzgk/std/newGbInfo?hcno=2DB552CAA58F589705C3DC7AD47AC2AB][GB 44495-2024]] is the current
Chinese vehicle-cybersecurity standard. [[https://unece.org/transport/documents/2021/03/standards/un-regulation-no-156-software-update-and-software-update][UN R156]]
addresses software-update management, a related lifecycle boundary.

The mission failure modes below come from the
[[https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.06996][mission-level runtime assurance study]];
[[https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.29114][ReasonBreak]] motivates testing an
untrusted VLA producer. The concrete risk rows, owner allocation, and
policy treatments are *this example's projection*, not official ratings
or controls prescribed by those sources. A real TARA still needs
item-specific damage scenarios, attack paths, impact and feasibility
ratings, risk decisions, and accountable approval.

* Treatment matrix

The same seven axes are used by
[[file:../Mission/SuccessorBoundary.lean][the executable mission matrix]].
=A= means the named root authorizes the command and =D= means it denies.
The =clear= row is a positive control, not a threat. These six roots are
composition stages for one illustrative vehicle policy, not six actual
vehicle models.

| TARA axis | Asset and damage scenario | Treatment owner | Base | Platform | Mission | Evidence | Integrated | Current |
|-----------+---------------------------+-----------------+------+----------+---------+----------+------------+---------|
| Future collision | Vehicle motion; physical harm | Platform | A | D | A | A | D | D |
| Lost checkpoint | Mission route; task cannot complete | Mission | A | A | D | A | D | D |
| Restricted region | Operating area; future entry | Mission | A | A | D | A | D | D |
| Budget exhaustion | Mission reserve; future route infeasible | Mission | A | A | D | A | D | D |
| Clear command | No modeled damage; positive control | Base | A | A | A | A | A | A |
| Unbound witness | Decision evidence; wrong proposal accepted | Evidence | A | A | A | D | D | D |
| Stale horizon | Decision evidence; old prediction reused | Current | A | A | A | A | A | D |

[[file:TaraProjection.lean][TaraProjection.lean]] ties these axes to
the verified 42-cell matrix. The underlying POO model declares six
owner edits. Six standalone Cedar revisions materialize 18 policy
occurrences; those runtime artifacts are *not* compressed away. The
compression is in the source of change: in the controlled fallback
maintenance experiment, one =Base= edit propagates through all six
roots, whereas six independently maintained Cedar snapshots require
six condition edits. Official Rust Cedar matches Lean on the 68
original decisions and 12 changed-case decisions. The
[[file:../Mission/README.org][mission case]] gives the exact scope and
limitations of that comparison.

* What this adds to TARA

A TARA work product identifies and rates a risk; it does not itself
execute a Cedar decision, bind a reference to a command, observe the
later trajectory, or calculate which proof certificates a policy edit
invalidates. Lean, Cedar, and Lean-POO add executable treatment,
checkable decision evidence, and owner-local version composition.
They do not replace the human risk judgment or establish regulatory
compliance by themselves. Modular TARA methods already exist: the
[[https://saemobilus.sae.org/articles/mosaic-tara-a-comprehensive-tara-methodology-automotive-cybersecurity-2026-26-0609][MOSAIC-TARA study]] explicitly addresses assessment reuse across ECUs
and vehicle platforms. The comparison here is with a standalone TARA
matrix and independently maintained Cedar snapshots, not a claim that
all TARA methods lack reuse.

For VLA-driven vehicles, a useful forward-looking TARA record should
also name the model and prompt provenance, command and horizon binding,
reference authority, observation authority, model-update revision, and
the evidence that a physical fallback was actually applied. The current
example implements only a small part of that record: ID and tick
binding, a source-backed offline trajectory comparison, hypothetical
post-decision observations, and policy-level fallback authorization.

* Decision and observation states

This diagram shows the intended integration boundary. The host owns
reference verification, sensor provenance, and physical handoff; the
example exposes the two Lean checks and Cedar authorization.

#+begin_src mermaid
stateDiagram-v2
    [*] --> Proposed
    Proposed --> Denied: Cedar denies command
    Proposed --> ReferenceCheck: Cedar allows command
    ReferenceCheck --> FallbackRequested: reference invalid or path outside envelope
    ReferenceCheck --> Executing: independent reference accepted
    Executing --> Executing: verified observation within envelope
    Executing --> FallbackRequested: observation invalid, late, or outside envelope
    FallbackRequested --> HostHandoff: Cedar authorizes fallback request
    HostHandoff --> [*]: host applies physical controller
    Denied --> [*]
#+end_src

The diagram is an interface contract, not a demonstrated physical
controller. In particular, comparing execution only with the attacked
VLA's own prediction can miss a self-consistent attack; the
[[file:../Mission/TrajectoryReplay.lean][Lean replay]] includes that
counterexample.
