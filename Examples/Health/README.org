* Clinical break-glass and patient preferences

[[file:ClinicalBreakGlass.lean][ClinicalBreakGlass.lean]] is an executable,
source-grounded model of patient-record access. It combines documented
features from two deployed EHR settings; it is not a transcription of either
hospital's entire production policy. The principal, patient, and facility
identifiers are fictive. The example does not contain patient data.

** Source-to-model map

| Published source | Observed behavior or specification | Lean representation and checked case |
|------------------+-------------------------------------+--------------------------------------|
| [[https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4265219/][Careweb design study]] | Patients could restrict display by provider or data category. Its break-glass control displayed that patient's hidden data for the current viewing session, and the system logged the event. | =restricted= is a deliberately coarse patient-preference projection. =patient-preference=, =expired-session=, =wrong-patient=, and =preferenceChangeConformsFully= exercise restriction, patient scope, session expiry, and a changed preference. |
| [[https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4265224/][Careweb prospective study]] | Providers used the control in a real clinic, and investigators recorded each break-glass event and subsequent data viewed. | The model requires a trusted =breakGlassGranted= fact but does not treat the fact itself as an audit record. |
| [[https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5333332/][French DMP deployment study]] | The shared record supported emergency access when the patient could not consent, required strong practitioner authentication, and exposed access logs to patients. | =cross-site-break-glass=, =patient-can-consent=, =weak-authentication=, and the shared-record entity model exercise those decision inputs. Patient incapacity, authentication, and patient-visible logging remain upstream or downstream facts. |
| [[https://hl7.org/fhir/R4/security-labels.html#6.1.1.3][HL7 FHIR R4 security labels]] | A break-glass request has a treatment purpose; the specification calls for authorization, consent checks, and an =AuditEvent=, while leaving local protocol rules to implementers. | =non-treatment-purpose= is denied. The model treats the session grant as application-projected context and does not claim FHIR interoperability or =AuditEvent= emission. |
| [[https://healthit.gov/test-method/emergency-access/][US HealthIT emergency-access test method]] | Emergency access is a pre-established, auditable capability of the health IT module. | The example asserts an access decision only. Audit recording is an explicit integration boundary. |
| [[https://docs.aws.amazon.com/verified-access/latest/ug/trust-data-third-party-trust.html][AWS Verified Access device trust context]] | A separate AWS access product can supply device-risk context to Cedar policies through configured trust providers. | =deviceTrusted= is a simplified extra veto in this model, not a claim about Careweb, DMP, or direct Verified Access integration. |

The split into Privacy, Security, and Emergency policy owners is a design
assumption for exploring composition. It is not reported as the organization
of any cited deployment.

** Why POO is needed

The clinical base, privacy rule, device rule, and emergency rule have separate
owners. A C4 diamond composes those branches once. The integrated module
overlays the emergency rule with patient, session, treatment-purpose, and
device conditions, then removes a legacy broad permit. The executable
=preIntegrationRiskDetected= theorem shows why the removal matters: before
integration, the inherited broad permit allows a cross-site read without a
break-glass grant; after integration it is denied.

=compileWithTrace= records all seven applied edits, including removal of the
legacy permit; its surviving-policy projection reports who introduced and
last edited each policy. These APIs close a diagnostics gap exposed by the
case: a materialized Cedar policy set alone did not explain why a permit was
absent or how an emergency policy reached the final set.
The patient-preference change alters entity data
without editing any policy module. =preferenceRevisionFootprintExact= proves
that the entity-validation obligation is selected while policy validation,
schema validation, and request validation are unaffected.

=privacyRevision= follows the Privacy to Integrated compilation. Its checked
=privacyRevisionBodies= receipt identifies the emergency overlay and device
veto as the two fresh policy bodies; =privacyRevisionReused= identifies the
routine permit and patient restriction as the two unchanged bodies.
=privacyCertificate= closes the Privacy
baseline once, =freshClinicalCertificates= checks only the two new bodies,
and =integratedCertificateFromPrivacy= derives the Integrated authorization
certificate through the Cedar adapter. The final soundness example consumes
that migrated certificate. This is proof reuse across a concrete clinical
policy evolution, not just a count of shared policy records.

Across Base, Privacy, Security, Emergency, and Integrated, separately stored
snapshots would repeat 15 policy records (2 + 3 + 3 + 3 + 4). The module
source defines six policy bodies and seven explicit edits. This counts
structural duplication in the example; it does not measure production LOC,
review time, or maintenance cost.

** Proof receipts and limits

[[file:ClinicalBreakGlassExport.lean][ClinicalBreakGlassExport.lean]] exports the
emergency and integrated POO roots through Cedar's public JSON format. The
official Cedar Rust authorizer checks fourteen Lean-computed requests,
including all twelve clinical cases, the inherited broad-permit risk, and a
released patient preference. It also reparses the generated
[[file:Policies/clinical-integrated.cedar][integrated Cedar policy file]]
and checks the same decisions and error counts. =just export-cedar-language=
regenerates both clinical revisions; =just check-cedar-language= detects stale
files or a semantic disagreement.

=scenarioConformsFully= checks all twelve named requests against the
compiled policy set, Cedar validation, Cedar authorization, and empty runtime
policy-error lists. =caseNamesUnique= prevents ambiguous case references.
=privacyRevisionBodies= and =integratedCertificateFromPrivacy= check the
specific incrementally reclosed authorization certificate.
=provenanceConformsFully= checks C4 order and policy origin;
=removalRecordedFully= checks the final removal event. A closed
authorization certificate transports Cedar's upstream soundness theorem for
the emergency request. These are machine-checked claims about this Lean
model and its finite scenario table. They do not establish that a real
hospital's policy, data projection, session service, or audit pipeline is
correct.

The application must authenticate the clinician, establish whether emergency
access is justified, bind the grant to a patient and session, verify device
posture, enforce Cedar's response, and write a durable audit event before
releasing data. Neither Cedar's Allow nor the proof certificate proves that
those external actions occurred. A later domain integration must supply and
verify those effects; the generic =cedar-poo-spec= adapter does not impersonate
an EHR or an AWS Verified Permissions service.

* Wearable observation to AI triage to clinician alert

[[file:WearableTriage.lean][WearableTriage.lean]] models a hospital remote-monitoring
workflow with two required decisions: an AI service may analyze a vital-sign
observation, then a clinician may release the resulting alert. All patients,
devices, services, and institutions in the model are fictive. The workflow is
an application design derived from the risks and data boundaries below, not
a transcription of a deployed hospital system.

** Source-to-model map

| Source | Published boundary | Model choice |
|--------+--------------------+--------------|
| [[https://www.fda.gov/science-research/artificial-intelligence-and-medical-products/fda-digital-health-and-artificial-intelligence-glossary-educational-resource][FDA digital-health and AI glossary]] | Its assistive-AI example includes a wearable monitoring vital signs and alerting a user or provider. | A fictive wearable observation enters an AI analysis step before a clinician releases an alert. The example does not evaluate alert accuracy. |
| [[https://hl7.org/fhir/us/core/STU7/scopes.html][HL7 US Core SMART scopes]] | Patient, user, and system scopes can limit access to Observation data, including the vital-signs category. | =analyze-vitals= permits only the vital-signs category and treatment purpose. OAuth scope and patient identity validation remain with the host. |
| [[https://hl7.org/fhir/R4/consent.html][HL7 FHIR R4 Consent]] | Consent provisions can identify actors, purposes, periods, security labels, and controlled data. | =consentActive= is a coarse, trusted projection on one Observation. The example does not parse or enforce a FHIR Consent resource. |
| [[https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/safety-communications/cybersecurity-vulnerabilities-certain-patient-monitors-contec-and-epsimed-fda-safety-communication][FDA patient-monitor safety communication]] | Certain connected monitors had vulnerabilities involving unauthorized control and data exfiltration; FDA described local-only monitoring or a patch as responses. | =DeviceSecurity= vetoes untrusted input. =Quarantined= isolates one fictive source while other devices remain usable; =Recovered= removes that policy after an externally verified remediation. |
| [[https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/marketing-submission-recommendations-predetermined-change-control-plan-artificial-intelligence][FDA AI device change-control guidance]] | AI-enabled device software changes require planned evaluation within the guidance's scope. | =ClinicalAI= adds a locally defined =modelApproved= gate. This Boolean is not a claim of FDA approval or regulatory compliance. |

The FDA safety communication concerns specific connected patient monitors,
not the fictive wearable here. It motivates the incident-response pattern;
the example does not claim those products or vulnerabilities are identical.

** POO scenario and checked behavior

=Base= permits vital-sign analysis and same-facility alert release. A legacy
baseline would even allow a withdrawn-consent observation. =Privacy= adds a
consent veto, =DeviceSecurity= adds a device-trust veto, and =ClinicalAI=
overlays the two inherited permits with model-approval and clinician-review
requirements. =Integrated= combines these independently owned branches using
C4. No branch repeats the other's policy body.

When a monitor source becomes suspect, =Quarantined= extends a single forbid.
The source's observation is denied at both flow layers; another monitor's
observation remains usable. =Recovered= removes that forbid without editing
the privacy, device-trust, or clinical AI branches. The checked compilation
equals the earlier =Integrated= policy set. The two revision receipts each
identify exactly one changed policy ID. Separately, changing a patient's
projected consent from withdrawn to active changes the result without a
policy edit.

The thirteen named flows include withdrawn consent, missing device trust,
unapproved AI model, missing clinician review, wrong facility and purpose,
wrong Observation category, quarantine, unaffected monitoring, and recovery.
The [[file:WearableTriageExport.lean][exporter]] supplies twenty-six individual
decisions to the official Rust Cedar authorizer and checks the four generated
[[file:WearablePolicies/integrated.cedar][Cedar revision artifacts]].

The host must establish patient identity and consent state, authenticate the
device and clinician, determine whether model and clinical review occurred,
bind both requests to the same observation, and enforce the two decisions
before releasing an alert. Cedar and POO decide authorization from those
facts; neither verifies device firmware, clinical accuracy, or an FDA status.
